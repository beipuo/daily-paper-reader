## ABSTRACT 

Discovery of causal relationships in high-dimensional Gene Regulatory Networks (GRN) is computationally challenging and often difficult to interpret due to dense connections. Therefore, grouping genes together into functional modules can im-prove tractability and biological interpretability. However, existing cluster level causal discovery methods assume access to a predefined admissible partition, re-quiring the graph over clusters to be acyclic. Constructing such partitions is there-fore challenging. In this work, we introduce GO-based Clustering for Causal Discovery ( GO4CD ), an algorithm that uses Gene Ontology (GO) to construct biologically meaningful gene partitions at multiple levels of granularity, while fa-voring those more likely to be admissible for causal discovery. GO4CD groups together genes participating in a shared biological process, and propagates gene annotations through the ontology hierarchy to achieve different granularity of par-titions. Furthermore, we integrate GO4CD with Causal Learning over Clusters (CLOC) algorithm and evaluate recovery of true Markov equivalence class both with an oracle of conditional independencies and on simulated gene expression data using multivariate conditional independence tests. We evaluate GO4CD on multiple E.coli regulatory subnetworks and find that it is inadmissible in 18.1% of the cases, compared with 65.3-82.3% for the semantic-similarity baselines. Our results indicate that GO4CD is substantially better suited to learning causal GRNs defined over biologically meaningful gene clusters. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Causal discovery aims to discover cause-and-effect relationship between variables and therefore finds application across multiple domains (Pearl et al., 2003). Understanding the physical and chemical interactions within a cell is a critical step for developing targeted therapies for complex diseases (Tejada-Lapuerta et al., 2025). These interactions are typically modeled using Gene Reg-ulatory Networks (GRNs), which map genes as nodes and their regulatory relationships as directed edges. While traditional methods frequently infer GRNs by fitting models to gene co-expression, these approaches increasingly produce correlational ”hairballs” that fail to capture true molecular 1arXiv:2610.06042v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 causation (Maizels & Briscoe, 2026). Inferring causal GRNs where edges strictly represent mecha-nistic cause-and-effect relationships has become a central challenge in computational genomics. While gene knockout experiments are capable of uncovering direct causal relationships, inferring a complete causal network at the individual gene level is practically infeasible due to the sheer volume of combinatorial interventions required (Scheines, 2001). Furthermore, transcriptomics datasets suf-fer from the curse of dimensionality, where the number of measured genes outnumbers the available samples (Magwene & Kim, 2004). Consequently, the dimensionality of the structural search space is astronomically large; even a small network of just ten genes possesses over 10 30 possible network topologies (Maizels & Briscoe, 2026). The large number of genes (approx. 20,000 in humans) (Con-sortium, 2004) and the dense connections between them make recovering exact causal connections in GRNs statistically and computationally intractable, as well as difficult to interpret. Intuitively, however, biological systems do not operate as flat networks of isolated genes. Rather, they are or-ganized into functional modules and hierarchies that bridge the gap from molecular components to cellular phenotypes (Maizels & Briscoe, 2026). To address this, biologists coarse-grain the net-work to a “representational” level by grouping co-expressed genes into functional modules (Segal et al., 2003). Once genes are clustered, one can aim to identify a coarser causal diagram capturing relationships among broader biological mechanisms. Grouping variables into clusters is challenging because the resulting partitions must make biologi-cal sense while simultaneously satisfying the strict assumptions of causal discovery. A major bot-tleneck here is the concept of admissibility , which requires the resulting graph between clusters to be entirely acyclic . This does not naturally follow from the gene network being acyclic, since two clusters can each contain an ancestor of a gene in the other, thus closing a cycle that never existed between individual genes. To find these admissible partitions, state-of-the-art methods depend on fully specified Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) over all variables (Tikka et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2025). Others that learn from data do so under overly restrictive assumptions such as interventional data (Chalupka et al., 2016), linear functional forms (Massidda et al., 2024) or the absence of latent confounders (Niu et al., 2022). Other methods (Ruiz de Vargas et al., 2026; Anand et al., 2026) simply assume the clusters are already provided. Satisfying these requirements from data alone is fundamentally difficult across domains, but it is especially a problem in biology. To tackle this open problem, we introduce GO-based Clustering for Causal Discovery (GO4CD) ,an algorithm that leverages prior knowledge from the Gene Ontology (GO) to partition the genes into clusters with biological meaning which are more likely to be admissible for causal discovery. Using the ontology hierarchy, GO4CD can construct these partitions at different levels of granularity. GO4CD achieves this by grouping genes based on shared biological processes and systematically propagates these annotations through the ontology hierarchy. GO4CD explicitly injects established biological priors to guide the abstraction process, making it far less likely to group genes in a way that creates spurious cluster-level cycles. We then integrate GO4CD with the Causal Learning over Clusters (CLOC) (Anand et al., 2026) algorithm to evaluate CLOC’s performance under two set-tings: oracle mode which guarantees perfect Conditional Independence (CI) assessment and on the gene expression data using multivariate CI tests. We test CLOC under oracle mode to report its performance solely based on inadmissible partitions by eliminating the effect of imperfect CI test, since under admissible partition and perfect independence test, CLOC guarantees recovery of the 

Markov equivalence class (MEC) as it is sound and complete. Furthermore, we also test CLOC on simulated gene expression data using multivariate CI tests where the errors are compounded from imperfect CI tests and inadmissible partitions for practical application. Extensive evaluations on E. coli regulatory subnetworks demonstrate that GO4CD’s partitions are inadmissible in only 18 .1% of the cases, against 65 .3% to 82 .3% for standard semantic-similarity baselines. To our knowledge, no existing method reports whether the partitions it uses are admissible. Ultimately, our results show that GO4CD is much better equipped to learn causal GRNs across biologically meaningful gene groups. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Causal Abstraction and Discovery over Variable Clusters Causal abstraction asks when low-and high-level causal models are consistent under corresponding interventions (Geiger et al., 2025; Rubenstein et al., 2017; Beckers & Halpern, 2019). Work on clusters either takes the partition as 2given or constructs it. Anand et al. (2023) and Anand et al. (2026) require a supplied admissible 

partition, inducing no directed cycles between clusters, while G¨ obler et al. (2025) assume prede-fined groups and an acyclic group-level model. Ninad et al. (2025) optimize aggregation within predefined groups. Ruiz de Vargas et al. (2026) use a supplied C-DAG, edges included, as back-ground knowledge for variable-level causal discovery. Where partitions or abstraction mappings are learned, the methods differ in their required inputs. Niu et al. (2022) and Rajpal et al. (2026) build clusters from observational data. Massidda et al. (2024) learn linear abstraction mappings to spec-ified high-level variables using paired low and high-level observations. Xia & Bareinboim (2024) learn abstract neural causal models using supplied structural knowledge. Zennaro et al. (2023) learn domain mappings between supplied causal models using interventional distributions. Tikka et al. (2023) and Zeng et al. (2025) instead cluster a known causal DAG, the former preserving causal-effect identifiability. In closely related work, Madaleno et al. (2026) construct partitions and acyclic cluster-level graphs from observational and interventional data with unknown intervention targets. GO4CD builds gene partitions from GO knowledge without using expression data or requiring a known variable-level causal graph. 

Gene Ontology and Semantic-Similarity-Based Clustering Semantic-similarity-based cluster-ing is widely used in natural language processing (Pereira et al., 1993; Slonim & Tishby, 2000) and has also been applied in biology. GO (Ashburner et al., 2000) provides a structured vocabulary and gene-product annotations for biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Semantic-similarity measures are developed to quantify functional relatedness. Resnik (1995) relies on the most informative common ancestor, while Lin et al. (1998) normalize the shared information from the compared terms. Wang et al. (2007) use ancestor contributions and GO relation types to derive similarities between GO terms and genes. A separate line of work represents GO terms and genes as embeddings, derived either strictly from ontology structure (Edera et al., 2022) or jointly with gene annotations (Smaili et al., 2018). We do not compare against these directly since the two sources they learn from are the same ones our baselines use in closed form, the ontology’s structure in Wang et al. (2007) and annotation frequencies in Resnik (1995) and Lin et al. (1998). Introducing embeddings would merely test a parameterized approximation of the same fundamental ontology signals already represented in our study. 

## 3 PRELIMINARIES 

In this section, we first review the basic concepts of causal directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), then their abstracted form cluster-DAG (C-DAG) and finally the Causal Learning Over Clusters (CLOC) algorithm. We assume the underlying causal structure is a standard DAG, G(V, E ), over a set of variables V.In a nutshell, constraint based causal discovery algorithms look for marginal/conditional indepen-dencies to prune and orient the graph. These (in)dependencies are encoded through d-separation .We operate under faithfulness assumption, which states that any independencies in the data imply separation in the graph. However, under observational data setting, certain graphs cannot be dis-tinguished since they carry the same independencies, for e.g. X → Z → Y , X ← Z → Y and 

X ← Z ← Y all say only that X and Y are independent given Z. DAGs that share a skeleton and the same v-structures form a MEC, and observational data identifies the causal structure only up to that class. The class is drawn as a Completed Partially Directed Acyclic Graph (CPDAG), which directs the edges every member agrees on and leaves the rest undirected. To formalize the structural abstraction of a DAG, we build upon the framework introduced by Anand et al. (2026). We first define how a DAG translates into a C-DAG below. 

Definition 1 (Cluster DAG (C-DAG) (Anand et al., 2026)). Let G(V, E ) be a DAG over a vertex set V, and let C = {C1, . . . , C k} be a partition of V. Construct a graph GC (C, E C ) over C with edge set EC defined as follows: an edge Ci → Cj is in EC if and only if there exist Vi ∈ Ci and 

Vj ∈ Cj such that Vi ∈ Pa( Vj ) in G. If GC (C, E C ) contains no directed cycles, we say that C is an 

admissible partition of V, and we call GC a cluster DAG , or C-DAG, compatible with G.

Anand et al. (2026) also introduced a new graphical representation to accurately represent the (in)dependencies over a C-DAG, formally known as αC-DAG. Similar to DAG, αC-DAG are re-coverable up to a MEC known as αC-CPDAG. Under faithfulness assumption, one can recover the 

αC-CPDAG using the Causal Learning over Clusters (CLOC) algorithm, given an admissible parti-tion (Algorithm 1 in Anand et al. (2026)). The CLOC algorithm operates in three phases: separating, marking and the orientation phase. In the separation phase, it removes edges for a pair of nodes X

and Y if it finds a separation set S such that ( X ⊥⊥ Y | S). In the second phase, marks are added over the triplets to encode (in)dependencies and finally the orientation phase which orients edges. 

Remark 2. Whether a partition is admissible depends on G, the DAG over V, which is unknown. To check it one would need the edges of G, and learning those is what clustering is meant to avoid. CLOC neither tests the condition nor constructs a partition that satisfies it, rather it assumes an admissible partition to be handed over. 

This constitutes a significant bottleneck in applying CLOC to real-world scenarios. In Section 5, we introduce our core contribution, a methodology to construct admissible partitions using the back-ground knowledge described in Section 4, which we then apply to the CLOC algorithm. 

## 4 BACKGROUND KNOWLEDGE 

Remark 1 and 2 open the problem of forming non-trivial admissible partition. Given a set of genes, there are many ways to partition them, from every single gene as its own cluster, to all of them collapsed to a single blob. We use Gene Ontology to restrict the space of clusters, which we describe in this section, as a necessary pre-requisite for our clustering algorithm. 

Gene Ontology (GO). GO is a standardised, structured and species-agnostic representation of bio-logical knowledge (Ashburner et al., 2000; The Gene Ontology Consortium, 2026). It is organised in three parts: Molecular Function (MF), Cellular Component (CC) and Biological Process (BP). 

As the name suggests, MF are activities performed by either a single or multiple gene products, and CC refer to the locations where these activities are performed. BPs are large, complex bio-logical (or gene) programs made up of multiple MFs. For example, the MF term cyclin-dependent protein kinase activity is part of the BP regulation of cell cycle . We use only BP in this work, be-cause it describes gene programs, and from here on we use the words term and biological process 

interchangeably. 

Relations in GO. GO arranges these terms as a DAG, where the terms are the nodes and the relations between them are the edges. The backbone is made of is a and part of relations: A is a Bmeans A is a type of B, and A part of B means A is a sub-process of B, so A never occurs outside B. The parent terms are broader than the child terms, which are more specialised. Unlike a strict hierarchy, where a child has only a single parent, GO allows a child to have more than one parent. For example, autophagosome assembly has two parents, organelle assembly and autophagosome organization . Biological Process is the root of this branch, and all the other BP terms like biological regulation and cellular process sit below it. On top of this backbone GO also records regulates 

relations, which connect one process to another it controls. These do not follow the hierarchy, and they are the source of the edges between our clusters. 

Granularity in GO. As one goes down a branch the terms become more specific, referring to a specialised type of BP. Since GO is a DAG, a term can have multiple parents and thus multiple paths to the root node. On top of this, not all parents sit at the same distance from the root node. Thus different paths to the root node can have different lengths. We use length of the longest such paths as its depth . For autophagosome assembly ’s two parents, organelle assembly sits at depth 5 and 

autophagosome organization at depth 6, so its depth is 6. In GO, some BPs are far more studied than others and have therefore been broken down into many child terms. For example, regulation of metabolic process and ferroptosis both sit at depth 4, but the former has 2,405 terms below it while the latter is a leaf. This means that two terms sitting at the same depth can still describe processes 4of very different breadth. We nonetheless use depth as our measure of specificity, and we state this as an assumption rather than as a property of GO. 

Assumption 1 (Depth as specificity). For any two terms T and T ′ with depth( T ) = depth( T ′),

T and T ′ are equally specific. 

Gene annotations in GO. A biological process is carried out by a set of genes acting together, and genes/gene products are connected to GO terms via annotations. A gene is annotated to BPs mainly with two labels: involved in , if it plays an integral role in that process, that is, the gene is a 

participant of the term; and acts upstream of , if its activity occurs upstream of the process, that is, the gene is a regulator of the term. In summary, GO terms represent distinct biological processes, their hierarchical depth indicates their level of specificity, regulation relations encode the interactions between these processes, and annotations map individual genes to the processes in which they participate.

Fixing a depth fixes the granularity, but it does not hand us a partition. At a given depth, a gene can take part in several terms, so their participant sets overlap. ## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

We investigate two core questions: how often GO4CD produces admissible partitions, and whether CLOC achieves better structural recovery on these partitions compared to semantic baselines. We evaluate CLOC in two settings. In oracle mode, we derive (in)dependencies directly via d-separation on the underlying gene-level DAG. In data mode, dependencies are inferred through conditional independence (CI) tests on expression data, which introduces statistical noise. To support this second mode, we extend the standard variable-level CLOC implementation to a multivariate setting. 66.1 SETUP 

Networks and Sampling. Our evaluation relies on a curated E. coli gene regulatory network from RegulonDB (Salgado et al., 2023). We strip out all feedback edges, so the gene-level graph is acyclic and any cluster-level cycle we report comes from the coarsening rather than from a loop we inherited. From this network we extract nested random subnetworks at seven sizes ( 50 to 1000 

genes) with five seeds per size, giving 35 subnetworks, each grown outward from a random seed gene following Marbach et al. (2009) (Appendix B). RegulonDB holds only 1781 genes, so the replicates overlap as they grow, sharing 2 genes at size 50 and 839 at size 1000 (details in Appendix B). 

Baselines. We compare against three semantic-similarity measures that read the same GO anno-tations as GO4CD but parse them differently. Resnik (Resnik, 1995) scores a gene pair by the information content of their most informative common ancestor, Lin (Lin et al., 1998) normalises that score, and Wang (Wang et al., 2007) ignores annotation counts and instead propagates a dis-counted score through a term’s ancestors. All three return a gene-by-gene similarity matrix rather than a partition, so we cluster each one with average linkage and cut the dendrogram at the k that GO4CD produces at that depth (details in Appendix A) . 

Comparison Criteria. We cluster each subnetwork across every depth of the ontology. To ensure comparisons are made at identical granularities, the baseline methods are constrained to match the exact cluster count generated by GO4CD at the corresponding depth. Comparisons are conducted strictly instance-wise, where an instance is defined by a fixed subnetwork size ( n), replicate, and ontology depth. Consequently, all four methods operate on the exact same set of genes and output the same number of clusters ( k). In total, this yields 288 distinct instances (e.g., an n = 50 subnetwork evaluated at depth 7 and partitioned into k = 20 clusters represents one instance). We discard instances where k = n, as each gene trivially forms its own cluster, collapsing the C-DAG back to the inherently acyclic gene-level DAG and yielding identical results across all methods. 

Dataset Generation. We utilized CausalRegNet (Kovaˇ cevi´ c et al., 2024) to generate realistic gene expression data for all subnetworks based on their underlying acyclic GRNs. We selected this model because it scales efficiently, copes with the issue of varsortability, and produces data that is both 

faithful to the underlying graph and consistent with the key distributional properties of real-world data. Full generation details are available in Appendix C. 

CI test. Tests at the cluster level are multivariate, since a cluster carries as many dimensions as it has genes. Kernel tests are too slow at this scale, so we use the Generalised Covariance Measure (Shah & Peters, 2020) instead, which regresses X and Y on Z and asks whether the residuals stay uncor-related. It applies at any dimension and needs no kernel matrix. We use its multivariate form with a Gaussian multiplier bootstrap, and take residuals from random forests with min samples leaf 

set to 10, and test at a significance level of 0.05 .

Implementation Details. Due to computational constraint, we run CLOC only on instances with at most 60 clusters in the oracle mode, and in data mode only on the three smallest network sizes ( 50 ,

100 and 200 genes) with at most 20 clusters and separating sets of size at most 2. 6.2 ADMISSIBILITY OF THE CONSTRUCTED PARTITIONS 

GO4CD relies entirely on GO as prior knowledge, enabling its application to networks with un-known regulatory edges. However, because GO does not annotate every gene at every depth, the extent to which this knowledge guides GO4CD toward an admissible partition remains an empirical question, which we evaluate here. 

GO4CD leads with more admissible partitions than any baseline across all subnetworks. In Table 1, we report the percentage of instances whose C-DAG is cyclic. Each row pools the five replicates and the depths where all the methods return the same k, and the last row pools every size. Overall, GO4CD is inadmissible on just 18.1% of instances while all other baselines range between 

65 .3% –82 .3% . GO4CD also stays the lowest at every size. Even at smaller sizes of 50 and 100 ,where the five replicates share only 2 and 47 genes respectively, GO4CD is inadmissible on 10 .0% 

and 13 .2% of instances against 32 .5–52 .6% for the baselines. 

The GO-informed gene distribution explains the admissibility. To test whether GO4CD’s par-tition admissibility stems from its cluster size distribution or its GO-informed gene placement, we 7introduce a Rand baseline that randomly assigns genes while strictly preserving GO4CD’s exact cluster sizes (averaged over ten draws). While Rand perfectly mimics GO4CD’s structural shape, it remains cyclic 80 .9% of the time, producing only 3 completely acyclic instances out of 288 .Performance-wise, Resnik (R) aligns with Rand , Lin (L) and Wang (W) exceed it by 15 and 12 per-centage points respectively, and GO4CD outperforms it by 63 points. This confirms that admissibil-ity is driven by GO’s gene grouping rather than partition topology (see Table 4 in Appendix D.1.1). As shown in Table 1, GO4CD typically concentrates genes into one dominant cluster alongside many singletons, whereas baselines produce more mid-sized clusters and significantly more C-DAG edges (averaging 253 –307 versus GO4CD’s 111 ). Although fewer edges reduce the likelihood of cycles, this topology alone does not guarantee admissibility. Despite sharing GO4CD’s exact dominant-cluster distribution, Rand retains 278 edges—exceeding most baselines. Thus, GO4CD is admissi-ble not because a single large cluster trivially absorbs random edges, but because GO successfully groups interacting co-participant genes into the same block. 

Missing annotations and unrecorded edges in GO account for all inadmissible instances. 

Across all sizes and replicates, GO4CD produced 52 inadmissible instances encompassing 482 

edges within their SCCs (see Table 5 in Appendix D.1.1 for full details). In every case, this was caused by missing data in GO, stemming from two distinct sources: missing gene annotations at the evaluated depth (meaning GO could not identify the corresponding incoming or outgoing edges), or regulatory edges were simply absent from GO knowledge base despite the presence of annotations. Of the 52 instances, 9 were strictly due to absent edge data between fully annotated nodes. Notably, all 9 were 2-cluster loops involving the cytoplasmic translation cluster. The remaining 43 instances exhibited a mix of annotated and unannotated genes. ## 7 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Causal discovery over high-dimensional GRNs is computationally intractable and difficult to inter-pret at the single-gene resolution. Recent methods address this by abstracting or clustering genes, but operate under strict assumption of admissible partition provided to them. We bridge this gap with GO4CD, an algorithm that uses knowledge from GO to abstract genes into biologically meaningful clusters along the ontology hierarchy. On E. coli subnetworks from RegulonDB, GO4CD yielded admissible partitions far more often than the baselines, and a size-preserving random control tied this to the ontology’s structure rather than the partition’s shape. We integrated GO4CD with CLOC algorithm and reported its performance using oracle and multivariate CI test. For future work, we aim to use neuro-symbolic methods to learn partitions and then apply constraint based test for causal discovery on them.

## B SAMPLING SUBNETWORKS FROM REGULON DB 

Source network and sampling. We based our sampling on the E. coli regulatory network from RegulonDB. After filtering for high-confidence edges and removing feedback loops, we were left with an acyclic network containing 1,781 genes and 3,416 edges. Because this network is discon-nected, 1,699 of those genes form a single giant component, while the remaining 82 are scattered across 26 isolated fragments (none larger than 12 genes). If we started a sampling walk inside one of these small fragments, it would quickly hit a dead end, forcing the walk to artificially jump to unconnected genes. To prevent this, we seed every walk inside the giant component, guaranteeing that all extracted subnetworks remain fully connected. 

Subnetwork extraction. To extract a subnetwork of size n, we start by selecting a random seed gene from the giant component and grow the subnetwork iteratively, adding one gene at a time. At each step, we identify all neighboring genes—defined as any gene in the source network with a direct edge to the current subnetwork. We count how many connections each neighbor shares with the subnetwork and add the one with the highest count, breaking ties randomly. Edge directions are ignored during this selection process because a gene is structurally attached whether it regulates a gene inside the subnetwork or is regulated by one. However, all original edge directions are preserved in the final extracted network. 

Comparison to previous methods. Our overall procedure closely follows the module extraction method of Marbach et al. (2009), with one key difference regarding this selection criterion. Mar-bach et al. select the neighbor that maximizes the subnetwork’s modularity Q, defined as the actual number of internal edges minus the expected number of edges in a randomized network with an identical degree sequence: 

Q = 14m s⊤Bs, Bij = Aij − kikj

2m (1) 13 where Aij is the edge count between genes i and j, ki is the degree of gene i, m is the total number of edges, and s is the subnetwork indicator vector ( si = 1 if gene i is included, −1 otherwise). Instead of using modularity, our rule relies solely on the raw number of edges connecting the neigh-bor to the subnetwork. The expected edge penalty ( kikj 

> 2m

) in Q disproportionately penalizes high-degree genes; if a gene regulates a large portion of the network, it is statistically expected to have edges pointing into any subnetwork, meaning those edges count for very little under the modularity metric. By dropping the penalty and counting all edges fully, our method incorporates these crucial global regulators into the subnetwork much earlier. 

Growth runs and replicates. For each replicate, we perform a single growth run up to 1,000 genes. Smaller subnetwork sizes are taken as prefixes of this run, meaning the 50-gene network is nested within the 100-gene network, which is nested within the 200-gene network, and so forth. For each target size, we extract the induced subgraph from the source network, retaining only the existing edges between the selected genes. Because an induced subgraph of an acyclic network inherently remains acyclic, we do not need to perform any additional cycle-breaking. Ultimately, we generated 35 subnetworks by extracting seven different sizes (ranging from 50 to 1,000 genes) across five replicates seeded with the genes uvrB , torD , trxB , ttdB , and gatA . The resulting edge counts range from 128–212 for the 50-gene networks to 2,612–2,623 for the 1,000-gene networks. Finally, because we simulate expression separately, we use this entire procedure strictly to establish the network topology. 

## C DATA GENERATION 

Why we chose CausalRegNet. We generated all expression data with CausalRegNet (Kovaˇ cevi´ cet al., 2024), an explicit structural causal model for single-cell counts. CausalRegNet draws each gene once from its parents and its own independent noise, and it never integrates a trajectory over time, so its sampling distribution is Markov to the input DAG by construction. Simulators like SER-GIO (Dibaeinia & Sinha, 2020) work the other way round, since they evolve a stochastic differential equation to a pseudo steady state and then sample from it, and the shared dynamics introduce depen-dencies the DAG never licensed. We confirmed this difference on the 21-gene E. coli SOS network, where SERGIO’s data was not Markov to its own GRN. On the same network all six conditional independence tests agreed with the graph once we drew at least 20,000 cells from CausalRegNet, which is why we generate well above that count.

Edge signs and weights. Every edge needs a signed weight, and we took the sign from Regu-lonDB’s (Salgado et al., 2023) own function annotation rather than assigning it ourselves. An edge marked as an activator gets a positive sign and one marked as a repressor gets a negative sign. We then drew the magnitude independently per edge from U (1 , 3) .14 We drew the magnitude randomly to avoid an artifact we had already run into. If every activator carries one fixed magnitude and every repressor carries its negative, then a gene with an equal number of activators and repressors receives exactly zero net input, so its regulators leave no trace in the data. This case is common in RegulonDB rather than rare. Across our 35 subnetworks, 2,087 of the 14,732 regulated genes (14.2%) have exactly as many activators as repressors, counting a gene once for each subnetwork it appears in. On the 26-gene SOS-extended network this cancellation was the confirmed cause of a cluster edge that we could not recover. Drawing continuous magnitudes makes an exact cancellation a probability-zero event, and it does so without us tuning any individual gene. 

Edges with no usable sign. RegulonDB records some edges with conflicting activation and repres-sion evidence, and a few with no sign character at all. We assign each of these a random sign from the same random stream. Across the 35 subnetworks this affects 4,018 of 49,154 edges (8.2%), of which 16 are unsigned and the rest carry dual evidence. These edges keep their position in the graph but not their recorded direction of effect, so our simulated data follows the graph we score against while departing from RegulonDB’s regulation semantics on this small fraction of edges. 

What we generated. We simulated 100,000 cells for each of the 35 subnetworks. Every run starts from seed 2026, so a run reproduces exactly, and the datasets differ because the networks do, not because the seeds do. Each dataset is generated from the same acyclic gene-level DAG that we coarsen to obtain the ground-truth C-DAG, so the data and the ground truth come from one structure rather than two. We check acyclicity before simulating and stop if the check fails. All randomness comes from a single seeded stream consumed in a fixed order, first the signs of the ambiguous edges, then the weight magnitudes, then μ, then θ, and finally the simulation draws, so a given seed reproduces a dataset end to end.
